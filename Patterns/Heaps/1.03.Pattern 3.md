# Heap Pattern 3: Two Heaps (Balance / Running Median)

*A focused deep-dive on the pattern that introduces a genuinely new idea — keeping two live heaps in balance with each other, not just maintaining one. What it is, how to recognize it, how to derive the approach on an unseen problem, and a curated question list with direct links.*

---

## 1. What This Pattern Actually Is

Strip away the problem-specific language, and this pattern always reduces to one sentence:

> **"I need to know the middle of a data set that keeps changing — and I need that answer instantly, every time, without re-sorting everything from scratch."**

The "middle" here almost always means the **median**, though the same machinery shows up anywhere you need to split a live set into a "lower half" and an "upper half" and keep them balanced as data arrives.

Why a single heap doesn't work: a heap only ever gives you fast access to *one* extreme — the very smallest or very largest element. The median isn't an extreme; it's whatever sits in the *middle*. You can't get to the middle of a heap in less than linear time, because a heap only guarantees ordering between a parent and its children, not a full sorted order.

The fix is to use **two heaps together**, each responsible for one half of the data, so that the boundary between them — which is exactly where the median lives — is always sitting at the top of one or both heaps, ready in `O(1)`.

---

## 2. How to Recognize It in an Unseen Problem

Ask these questions the moment you finish reading a problem:

**Q1: Does the problem mention "median" explicitly, or ask for some notion of "the middle value" of a changing data set?**
If yes, this is close to a dead giveaway for this pattern.

**Q2: Do I need to repeatedly answer this "middle" question after every new piece of data arrives**, rather than just once at the end?
This is the detail that rules out simpler approaches. If you only needed the median *once*, you could just sort the final array and index into the middle — `O(n log n)` one time, done. It's the *repeated*, *live* nature of the question (median after every insertion) that makes re-sorting from scratch too expensive and pushes you toward maintaining a structure incrementally.

**Q3: Can I imagine "splitting" the data into two halves — a lower half and an upper half — where I only ever care about the values right at the boundary between them?**
If the problem's real need is "the largest of the small ones" and "the smallest of the large ones" (which is exactly what defines a median), you're confirmed in this pattern.

If Q1 and Q2 both fire, you're almost certainly here. Q3 is the mental model that tells you *how* to structure the solution once you know you're in this pattern.

---

## 3. The Core Idea, Explained Slowly

1. Maintain two heaps:
    - A **max-heap** holding the **smaller half** of the numbers seen so far. Its top is the *largest* element among the small half.
    - A **min-heap** holding the **larger half** of the numbers seen so far. Its top is the *smallest* element among the large half.
2. Keep them **balanced in size** at all times — either exactly equal, or the max-heap (lower half) allowed to hold exactly one more element than the min-heap. This one-extra-allowed-in-the-lower-half convention is arbitrary but standard; pick one and stick to it consistently.
3. **Why this gives you the median instantly:** the max-heap's top is the biggest of the "small" numbers, and the min-heap's top is the smallest of the "large" numbers. Those two values are your immediate neighbors around the true midpoint of the sorted data — that's *by definition* what a median is. If the heaps are equal in size, the median is the average of both tops; if the max-heap has one extra element, the median is just its top.
4. **Insertion logic:** when a new number arrives, don't just drop it into whichever heap "seems right" — route it, then rebalance:
    - Push it into the max-heap (lower half) first.
    - Immediately pop the max-heap's top and push it into the min-heap (upper half) — this guarantees every element that ends up staying in the max-heap really is `≤` everything in the min-heap.
    - If the min-heap has now grown larger than the max-heap is allowed to be smaller than (i.e., sizes are out of the agreed balance), move the min-heap's top back into the max-heap.

This "push, then shuffle across if needed" routine — rather than a single `if number < median push here else push there` check — is important: without it, you can end up with an unbalanced or incorrectly-ordered split. Route through one fixed heap first, then let the rebalancing step correct any imbalance.

## 4. The Template

```java
import java.util.*;

class MedianFinder {
    // lower half - max-heap (top = largest of the small numbers)
    private final PriorityQueue<Integer> lowerHalf = new PriorityQueue<>(Collections.reverseOrder());
    // upper half - min-heap (top = smallest of the large numbers)
    private final PriorityQueue<Integer> upperHalf = new PriorityQueue<>();

    public void addNum(int num) {
        // Step 1: always route through the lower half first
        lowerHalf.offer(num);

        // Step 2: shuffle the largest of the lower half into the upper half
        upperHalf.offer(lowerHalf.poll());

        // Step 3: rebalance - lowerHalf is allowed at most ONE more than upperHalf
        if (upperHalf.size() > lowerHalf.size()) {
            lowerHalf.offer(upperHalf.poll());
        }
    }

    public double findMedian() {
        if (lowerHalf.size() > upperHalf.size()) {
            return lowerHalf.peek(); // odd total count, lower half has the extra one
        }
        return (lowerHalf.peek() + upperHalf.peek()) / 2.0; // even total count
    }
}
```

Notice the fingerprint of this pattern: **every insertion touches both heaps** (push into one, immediately shuffle into the other, then possibly shuffle back), and **the answer always comes from peeking at one or both tops**, never from a `poll()`. Contrast this with Pattern 1 (evict permanently when over budget) and Pattern 2 (pop then push the next from the same source) — in this pattern, elements essentially never leave the two-heap system once they're in it; they only ever move *between* the two heaps.

## 5. Worked Example: Find Median from Data Stream

`https://leetcode.com/problems/find-median-from-data-stream/`

**Problem:** Design a data structure that supports adding integers one at a time and returning the median of all integers added so far, at any point.

**Recognizing the pattern:** "median," explicitly, plus "adding... one at a time" (Q1 and Q2 both fire immediately) — and there's no fixed `k` anywhere in sight, which also helps rule out Pattern 1.

**Reasoning to the approach:** this problem *is* the pattern in its purest form — there's no extra wrapping or disguise. Apply the template directly: a max-heap for the lower half, a min-heap for the upper half, insert by routing through the lower half and shuffling, and read the median off the tops.

```java
import java.util.*;

class MedianFinder {
    private final PriorityQueue<Integer> lowerHalf = new PriorityQueue<>(Collections.reverseOrder());
    private final PriorityQueue<Integer> upperHalf = new PriorityQueue<>();

    public void addNum(int num) {
        lowerHalf.offer(num);
        upperHalf.offer(lowerHalf.poll());

        if (upperHalf.size() > lowerHalf.size()) {
            lowerHalf.offer(upperHalf.poll());
        }
    }

    public double findMedian() {
        if (lowerHalf.size() > upperHalf.size()) {
            return lowerHalf.peek();
        }
        return (lowerHalf.peek() + upperHalf.peek()) / 2.0;
    }
}
```

**Complexity:** each `addNum` call does a constant number of heap operations, so it's `O(log n)` per insertion, and `O(1)` per `findMedian()` call — a huge improvement over re-sorting the entire dataset (`O(n log n)`) every time you need the median.

## 6. Common Mistakes Specific to This Pattern

- **Forgetting which heap is which.** The lower half needs a **max-heap** (so its top is the largest of the small numbers, right at the boundary), and the upper half needs a **min-heap** (so its top is the smallest of the large numbers). Mixing these up silently gives you nonsense results rather than a crash, which makes the bug easy to miss.
- **Checking `num < median` to decide which heap to push into directly**, instead of always routing through one heap first and rebalancing. This shortcut *seems* equivalent but is easy to get subtly wrong at the boundaries (especially with duplicate values) — the "always push into the lower half first, then shuffle" routine sidesteps this entirely and is worth doing by habit, not by case analysis.
- **Losing track of the size-balance invariant.** If you don't enforce "sizes are equal, or lower half has exactly one more" on *every single insertion*, the tops of your heaps quietly stop meaning what you think they mean, and the median calculation silently breaks.
- **Forgetting that `findMedian()` should peek, not poll.** Since elements never truly "leave" the system, popping during a median read (instead of during the next insertion's rebalancing) would corrupt your data structure for all future calls.

## 7. Related Questions — Practice These in Order

Work through these without looking at solutions first. Each should take under a minute to identify as Pattern 3 using Section 2's checklist.

1. **Find Median from Data Stream** — the foundational problem, walked through above; solve it again from scratch without peeking.
   `https://leetcode.com/problems/find-median-from-data-stream/`
2. **Sliding Window Median** — same two-heap idea, but now the window *slides*, meaning old elements must be *removed*, not just added. Since heaps don't natively support fast arbitrary removal, this forces you to learn the **lazy deletion** trick (mark an element as "stale" and skip over it when it eventually surfaces at the top, rather than removing it immediately) — a genuinely useful technique that reappears in Pattern 8 (graph algorithms) later in this series.
   `https://leetcode.com/problems/sliding-window-median/`
3. **IPO** — worth comparing side-by-side even though it's *not* actually a two-heap problem (it only needs one heap). People confuse it with this pattern because it also involves picking from a live-changing eligible set — solving it will sharpen your ability to tell "I need the middle of a live set" (this pattern) apart from "I need the best of a live-growing eligible set" (Pattern 1/4 territory).
   `https://leetcode.com/problems/ipo/`
