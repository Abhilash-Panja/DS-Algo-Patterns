# Heap Pattern 5: Greedy Simulation / Deferred Decision ("Push Now, Decide Later")

*A pattern that hides really well, because most problems in this family don't look like heap problems at all on first read — they read as simulations. What it is, how to recognize it, how to derive it on an unseen problem, and a curated question list with direct links.*

---

## 1. What This Pattern Actually Is

Strip away the problem-specific language, and this pattern always reduces to one sentence:

> **"I'm moving forward through time/distance/sequence, and I have a limited resource. I don't need to decide right now how to use each opportunity I encounter — I only need to decide once I'm actually forced to."**

The reason this is different from Patterns 1–4: in those patterns, the heap always holds **candidates for your final answer** or **representatives of your current best set**. In this pattern, the heap holds **options you haven't committed to yet** — you push things into it just because you *passed by* them, with no evaluation of whether you'll ever actually use them. You only reach into the heap and make a real decision at the exact moment you're forced to (you're about to run out of fuel, you're about to be unable to climb a building).

This "defer the decision until forced" habit is the single new idea this pattern introduces. Everything else — pushing, popping, comparators — reuses what you already know from Pattern 1.

---

## 2. How to Recognize It in an Unseen Problem

This pattern is genuinely harder to spot than the previous four, because the problem statement usually describes a **physical simulation** — a car, a person climbing buildings, a journey — with no mention of "top k," "merge," or "median" anywhere. Train yourself on these signals instead:

**Q1: Is there a notion of moving forward — through time, distance, or a sequence — where you can't go backward?**
Cars driving, people walking past buildings, days passing. This forward-only movement is the setup for "deferred decisions," because once you've passed an opportunity, it's gone unless you saved it.

**Q2: Do I have a limited, countable resource** (fuel, ladders, bricks, swaps, removals) **that I'm trying to use as efficiently as possible to go as far/long as I can?**
This resource constraint is what makes some choices better than others — you want to "spend" your limited resource on the moments where it helps the most, not necessarily the first moments you encounter.

**Q3: Would committing to a choice the instant I see it be short-sighted, because a better opportunity to use my resource might show up later — but I also can't just "wait and see" forever, because I might run out of resource before I decide?**
This is the actual crux. If committing immediately risks wasting your resource on a mediocre opportunity when a better one is coming, but waiting risks running out entirely — that tension is exactly what "push now, decide later" resolves. You don't decide immediately (avoids the first risk), but you also don't wait indefinitely (you decide the moment you're actually forced to, which avoids the second risk).

If Q1 and Q2 fire, and Q3 describes the tension you feel while reading the problem, you're in this pattern — even though nothing in the problem statement will ever say "heap."

---

## 3. The Core Idea, Explained Slowly

1. **Simulate moving forward** through the sequence (buildings, gas stations, days) one step at a time, exactly as the problem describes.
2. **Every time you encounter an "opportunity"** (a place where you *could* spend your limited resource), push its value into a heap — **regardless of whether you'll ever use it.** This is the "push now" half of the pattern, and it's the part that feels unnatural at first, because you're storing things you might never touch.
3. **Keep moving forward without spending anything**, as long as you can.
4. **The moment you're actually forced to make a decision** (you'd otherwise fail — run out of fuel, can't climb the next building) — *that's* when you reach into the heap. Pop the **best** deferred opportunity you've passed so far (the biggest fuel amount, the biggest height jump) and "retroactively" use your resource there instead of at the current moment.
5. **Why this is always at least as good as deciding immediately:** by construction, you're always retroactively spending your resource on the *best* opportunity you've seen up to this point — which is provably at least as good as whatever choice you'd have made by committing the instant you saw each opportunity. This is the greedy proof underlying the whole pattern, and it's worth being able to state out loud: **"never decide now what you can defer, and when forced to decide, pick the best option among everything you've deferred."**

The heap's role here is specifically to make step 5 cheap: without it, "find the best opportunity among everything I've passed so far" would require rescanning your whole history every single time you're forced to decide — the heap keeps that lookup at `O(log n)` instead.

## 4. The Template

```java
import java.util.*;

public int simulateWithDeferredDecisions(int[] opportunities, int resourceBudget) {
    PriorityQueue<Integer> deferredOptions = new PriorityQueue<>(Collections.reverseOrder()); // max-heap
    int resourceUsed = 0;

    for (int i = 0; i < opportunities.length; i++) {
        int cost = opportunities[i];

        // Step 2: push every opportunity you pass, unconditionally
        deferredOptions.offer(cost);
        resourceUsed += cost;

        // Step 4: only act when forced — here, "forced" means we've overspent our budget
        if (resourceUsed > resourceBudget) {
            // retroactively "undo" spending on the BEST (largest) deferred option seen so far
            resourceUsed -= deferredOptions.poll();
            // (conceptually: we're saying "I should have used my scarce/free resource here instead")

            // if we still can't recover, we've truly failed and must stop
            if (resourceUsed > resourceBudget) {
                return i; // furthest point reached
            }
        }
    }

    return opportunities.length; // reached the end
}
```

Notice the fingerprint of this pattern: **you push on every iteration, unconditionally, before you know if you'll need it** — unlike Pattern 1, where pushing is still unconditional but immediately followed by a size check; here there's no size cap on the heap at all. The heap only gets touched (popped) inside a **conditional branch that represents "I am now in trouble,"** not on a fixed schedule.

## 5. Worked Example: Furthest Building You Can Reach

`https://leetcode.com/problems/furthest-building-you-can-reach/`

**Problem:** You're given building heights, a number of bricks, and a number of ladders. Moving from building `i` to `i+1`: if the next building is shorter or equal, it's free. If it's taller, you must spend either `(height difference)` bricks, or one ladder, to climb it. Return the furthest building index you can reach using bricks and ladders optimally.

**Recognizing the pattern:** you're moving forward through a sequence of buildings (Q1 fires) with two limited resources, bricks and ladders (Q2 fires). Committing to "always use a ladder first" or "always use bricks first" the instant you see a climb is clearly short-sighted — you'd rather save your (free, but limited) ladders for the *biggest* climbs and pay bricks for the small ones — but you don't know which climbs are the biggest until you've seen them (Q3 fires).

**Reasoning to the approach:** treat ladders as your "free, unconditionally used resource" and think of them as *always* being used on the biggest climbs seen so far. Concretely: for every climb, spend a ladder unconditionally (push the climb size into a heap representing "climbs currently covered by a ladder"). The moment you've used more ladders than you actually have, you must convert the **smallest** ladder-covered climb (the one where a ladder was most "wasted") back into a brick payment instead — because that's the climb where losing the ladder costs you the least in bricks.

```java
import java.util.*;

class Solution {
    public int furthestBuilding(int[] heights, int bricks, int ladders) {
        // min-heap of climbs currently "covered" by a ladder
        PriorityQueue<Integer> ladderClimbs = new PriorityQueue<>();

        for (int i = 0; i < heights.length - 1; i++) {
            int climb = heights[i + 1] - heights[i];
            if (climb <= 0) continue; // no resource needed, descending or flat

            // Step 2: unconditionally "use a ladder" on every climb
            ladderClimbs.offer(climb);

            // Step 4: forced decision - we've used more ladders than we have
            if (ladderClimbs.size() > ladders) {
                // convert the SMALLEST ladder-covered climb into a brick payment instead
                bricks -= ladderClimbs.poll();
            }

            if (bricks < 0) {
                return i; // furthest building we could reach
            }
        }

        return heights.length - 1; // reached the very last building
    }
}
```

Notice this is a slightly inverted flavor of the general template: instead of pushing "opportunities I might spend a scarce resource on," we push "climbs I've provisionally covered with our *free* resource (ladders)," and evict the *smallest* of those back onto the *costly* resource (bricks) the moment we exceed our ladder count. Same "push now, decide later" spirit — just applied to a two-resource tradeoff instead of a single running total.

## 6. Common Mistakes Specific to This Pattern

- **Trying to decide "should I use my resource here?" immediately, at every opportunity**, instead of deferring. This usually leads to a much more complicated (and often incorrect) branching mess, because you don't yet have the information (what's coming later) to make that call correctly.
- **Forgetting that pushing is unconditional.** Every single opportunity — even ones you're pretty sure you won't use — gets pushed. Skipping "obviously bad" opportunities defeats the purpose, because your "obviously bad" judgment is exactly the premature decision this pattern avoids.
- **Popping from the heap on a fixed schedule** (like every `k`-th iteration) instead of **only when actually forced** (budget exceeded, resource exhausted). The eviction trigger in this pattern is a *feasibility check*, not a size cap — mixing this up with Pattern 1's fixed-size eviction is a common source of bugs.
- **Using the wrong heap type for "which deferred option to act on."** Ask explicitly: when forced to decide, do I want to retroactively use my scarce resource on the *biggest* deferred opportunity (as in the fuel-stops example) or *give up* my free resource on the *smallest* one (as in the ladders example)? Both directions show up across problems in this pattern — don't assume it's always a max-heap.
